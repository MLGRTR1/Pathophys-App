REGULATION OF METABOLIC PROCESSES
 Nervous system – autonomic nervous system.For example, stimulation of the sympathetic nervous system leads to
glycogenolysis.
 Liver, blood sugar regulation (glucose storage, glycogen formation and breakdown, gluconeogenesis), protein synthesis
(albumin, coagulation factors, plasma transport proteins, acute phase proteins, etc.), urea, lipids are stored in it, lipoproteins
are formed there, cholesterol is excreted or synthesized into bile acids, it is a storage site for many vitamins, etc. Their
detoxification function through hydroxylation reactions mediated by cytochromes P450 is significant, but it can be a source of
toxic metabolites.
 Digestive system – intake of water and food, its digestion and utilization, and excretion of certain substances. Lipoproteins
called chylomicrons are formed in the intestine. The intestinal microbiota also has a significant influence on metabolic
processes.
 Muscle mass is an important site of energy consumption (glucose, fatty acids, ketone bodies) and a reservoir of proteins in case
they are needed for the synthesis of other proteins (e.g., during inflammatory reactions or starvation). The main amino acid
released by muscles is glutamine.
 Adipose tissue is an important energy store in the form of fat droplets. In adipose tissue, androgens are converted to
estrogens, and many cytokines – adipokines – are produced that interfere with food intake (e.g., leptin) or metabolism
(adiponectin, resistin).
 Bones (storage of substances, calcium reserves, buffering capacity for hydrogen ions).
 Skin – thermoregulation, storage of subcutaneous fat, metabolism of certain substances (e.g., production of vitamin D).
 The kidneys help maintain water and mineral balance and activate vitamin D through hydroxylation. Their excretory
("cleansing") ability is important, as is their ability to reabsorb important substances that are filtered into primary urine
(glucose, amino acids).
 The respiratory and cardiovascular systems ensure an adequate supply of blood with oxygen and nutrients necessary for
metabolism, as well as the removal of metabolic waste products.

Hormones
 Insulin increases glucose uptake into muscle and adipose tissue cells by affecting
the membrane transporters for glucose GLUT4 and GLUT2 in hepatocytes. This
reduces blood glucose levels, stimulates protein synthesis, glycogenogenesis, and
lipogenesis, and inhibits lipolysis. Insulin suppresses appetite and induces the
production of its antagonists in terms of their effect on blood glucose levels.
 Glucagon increases blood glucose levels by increasing glycogenolysis and
gluconeogenesis. Its concentration rises physiologically during fasting and is
pathologically elevated in glucagonoma tumors.
 Growth hormone (STH; somatotropic hormone) has a proteoanabolic effect. It
increases glycemia by stimulating glycogenolysis and gluconeogenesis in the liver
(causingthe development of insulin resistance) and also has a lipolytic effect.
 Glucocorticoids mobilize energy resources for vital organs through catabolismof
proteins in muscles (and also in the skin) and lipolysis in subcutaneous fat. In the
liver, they have an anabolic effecton glycogen synthesis. They increase blood sugar
levels primarily by reducing glucose utilization(causing insulin resistance) and
stimulating glucogenesis in the liver. They are stress hormonesand are partly
responsible for metabolic changes that occur in response to stress, including insulin
resistance.

 Thyroxine (T4) and triiodothyronine (T3), which is several times more effective, cause an
overall increase in metabolism,increase oxygen consumption and heat production in
most organs, sensitize tissuesto the effects of catecholamines (thus promoting lipolysis),
and reduce blood cholesterol concentrations. Physiological concentrations have rather
proteoanabolic effects, but excessive amounts of thesehormones have a catabolic effect.
 Sex hormones have a predominantly anabolic effect, especially androgens (increased
muscle mass,positive nitrogen balance).
 Estrogens inhibit bone resorption, affect the subcutaneous distribution ofadipose tissue,
lead to sodium and water retention, and interfere with cholesterol metabolism
(protectiveeffect against atherosclerosis).
 Progesterone has a thermogenic effect (increase in body temperatureduring the luteal
phase of the ovarian cycle).
 Cytokines, tissue hormones, and neurotransmitters interfere with metabolism at many
levels.These include appetite regulators (e.g., leptin, neuropeptide Y),
gastrointestinalhormones (cholecystokinin, etc.), adipokines (cytokines produced in
adipose tissue), and inflammatorycytokines (e.g., IL-1, IL-6, TNF).

6. Vitamins
 The organism is dependent on the intake of vitamins or their precursors through food. Only small amounts of
some vitamins can be formed endogenously: vitamin D from steroid precursors, vitamin K and biotin by
intestinal microflora.
 For fat-soluble (A, D, E, K), maldigestion or malabsorption of fats leads to combined hypovitaminosis;
reserves in body fat (especially in the liver) prevent the development of deficiency symptoms for a long time
(this applies especially to vitamins A and D); with excessive intake, their accumulation occurs and toxic
manifestations of hypervitaminosis may occur.
 Water-soluble vitamins (B, C) are in minimal reserves in the organism and with excessive intake, they are
rapidly excreted by the kidneys and do not accumulate in the organism, on the contrary, hypovitaminosis
develops faster for the same reasons. The exception is vitamin B12, whose reserves in the liver are sufficient
for several years.
 Hypovitaminosis
 insufficient intake of the vitamin through food; e.g. vitamin D deficiency in breast milk, B1 deficiency in areas where
the main food is peeled rice, vitamin B12 deficiency
 insufficient endogenous vitamin production; e.g. intestinal dysmicrobia, lack of UV radiation
 impaired resorption (impaired emulsification of bile fats, malabsorption syndrome of various etiologies, selective
impaired resorption of vitamin B12 in pernicious anemia)
 congenital or acquired disorder of vitamin metabolism at the level of its storage, transport or conversion into the active
form (e.g. vitamin D-resistant rickets type I with a defect in the formation of 1,25-(OH)2-vitamin D3 in the kidneys)
 resistance to the vitamin (e.g. vitamin D-resistant rickets type II in the absence of the receptor for 1,25-(OH)2-vitamin
D3)
 increased need for the vitamin (during growth, pregnancy, hyperthyroidism, malignancies, etc.)

 Vitamin B2 (riboflavin)
 Riboflavin, as a part of the coenzymes flavin mononucleotide and flavin adenine dinucleotide, participates in a
number of oxidation-reduction reactions
 Vitamin B2 deficiency is associated with a deficiency of other vitamins and proteins-> developing countries
 In alcoholics and in patients with chronic infections or advanced malignancies.
 Deficiency is manifested by cheilosis (rhagades of the corners of the mouth, secondarily infected), atrophic

 source: meat, milk, eggs, yeast, vegetables
 Vitamin B6 (pyridoxin)
 This coenzyme is a cofactor for a number of transamination, carboxylation and deamination enzymes in amino
acid metabolism and glycogen, necessary for the production of serotonin
 Vitamin B6 is present in practically all foods, but it can be lost during their processing.
 Secondary hypovitaminosis occurs with long-term use of drugs that act as pyridoxine antagonists
(antituberculosis drug isoniazid, estrogens, penicillamine).
 Alcoholics suffer from vitamin B6 deficiency because acetaldehyde, potentiates the degradation of pyridoxine.
 The need for vitamin B6 is increased during pregnancy.
 The symptoms of vitamin B6 deficiency are similar to hypovitaminosis B2 – seborrheic dermatitis, cheilosis,
glossitis, peripheral neuropathy.

Body mass index (BMI)
Obesity is defined as a body mass index (BMI) that exceeds 30 kg/m2 and generally
develops when caloric intake exceeds caloric expenditure in genetically susceptible
individuals.
Waist-to-hip ratios is useful in visceral and peripheral obesity
Obesity is a risk factor for hypertension, stroke, hyperlipidemia, gallstones, nonalcoholic
steatohepatitis, gastroesophageal reflux, osteoarthritis, infectious disease, asthma, and
obstructive sleep apnea.

 psychogenic disorders of body image
 purposeful reduction in body weight by restricting food intake (sometimes associated with vomiting,

 chronic disease that leads to insufficient nutrient intake, secondary endocrine and metabolic

 obesity (abdominal);

 arterial hypertension;

=== PDF PAGE 85 ===
Lipoproteins
 Chylomicrons (CM) -formed in the intestine and transport absorbed exogenous
triacylglycerols. The main apoprotein is B48.
 VLDL (Very Low Density LP ,pre-ß-LP) - transport endogenous triacylglycerols synthesized in
the liver.
 IDL (Intermediate-Density LP) are formed from VLDL by the action of lipoprotein lipase and
have a lower content of triacylglycerols with a relatively higher proportion of cholesterol
esters.
 LDL (Low Density LP ,ß-LP) contain mainly cholesterol esters and a single apoprotein (B 100).
 HDL (High Density LP ,α-LP) reverse cholesterol transport. The main apoprotein is apoprotein
A. HDL cholesterol (HDL-C) is an important protective factor in atherosclerosis; its low
concentrations are one of the components of the metabolic syndrome.
 Lipoprotein(a), Lp(a) is a lipoprotein particle of LDL with a molecule very similar to
plasminogen attached. It is a significant risk factor for atherosclerosis. The liver is the key
organ for LP metabolism. Lipoproteins are produced in it (“intrinsic pathways”) and it is the
only organ capable of eliminating cholesterol from the body, either directly into bile or after
its conversion to bile acids. These are also part of bile, but undergo enterohepatic circulation.
In addition to the liver, lipoproteins are also produced in the intestine (“extrinsicpathways”).
 Molecules important for LP metabolism are apolipoproteins, enzymes and cell receptors.

Hypolipoproteinemia
Familial hypo-β-lipoproteinemia
 rare genetic abnormality, probably with autosomal dominant inheritance.
 ↓LDL cholesterol in plasma
 longevity, probably due to the low incidence of myocardial infarction
Abetalipoproteinemia
 rare autosomal recessive disease in which neither Apo-B48 is synthesized in enterocytes
nor apo-B100 in the liver.
 chylomicrons are absent in the circulation and the transport of endogenous cholesterol
to peripheral cells via LDL is impaired, concentration of cholesterol in plasma is reduced.
 Impaired absorption of lipids from the intestine causes their excessive loss in the feces
and thus a deficiency of fat-soluble vitamins.
 delayed development and reduced intelligence, polyneuropathy, and muscle weakness.
Hypoalphalipoproteinemia
 Conditions with reduced HDL-cholesterol have an increased risk of atherosclerosis and
the resulting cardiovascular disease. The familial form appears to have dominant
inheritance.
 The result is a relative lack of apoC-II and increased VLDL levels.
Analphaloproteinemia (Tangier disease)
 It is a rare disease with autosomal recessive inheritance, characterized by a complete
absence of HDL in plasma.
