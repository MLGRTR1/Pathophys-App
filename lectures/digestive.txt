Vomiting: chemoreceptor trigger zone
• The chemoreceptor trigger zone (the area postrema = a 
sensory circumventricular organ without a blood–brain 
barrier) has the receptors for dopamine, serotonin, 
histamine, substance P, opioid and acetylcholine 
receptors.
• These receptors monitor blood content and signal to 
the vomiting center. 
• They monitor intestinal, vagal, sympathetic input, 
including: pharyngeal stimulation, myocardial 
ischemia, gastric irritation or distension.
• Serotonin is produced by the intestinal 
enterochromaffin cells which can be stimulated by 
luminal distension or changed osmotic concentrations 
in intestinal contents.
• Serotonin antagonists are effective antiemetics
(drugs preventing or relieving nausea and 
vomiting) and have been used to treat nausea 
and vomiting associated with postoperative

Abdominal pain
• Parietal (somatic) pain arises from the parietal peritoneum. 
• More intense
• More localized : Nerve fibers from the parietal peritoneum travel 
with peripheral nerves to the spinal cord, and the sensation of 
pain corresponds to skin dermatomes
• Visceral pain arises from a stimulus (distention, inflammation, ischemia) 
acting on abdominal organ
• An dull (vague) rather than sharp, and is difficult to describe
• Poorly localized (diffuse):Pain is usually felt near the midline in 
the epigastrium, midabdomen, or lower abdomen. 
• Referred pain is visceral pain felt at some distance from a diseased 
or an affected organ. It is felt in the skin dermatomes or deeper 
tissues that share a central afferent pathway with the affected organ. 
Gallbladder pain is, for example, referred to the right shoulder or 
scapulae.

• Upper GI bleeding is bleeding in the esophagus, stomach, or duodenum and 
is characterized by 
• Hematemesis: is the vomiting of blood, which may be obviously red or 
have an appearance similar to coffee grounds.  
• Melena: dark, grainy digested blood (“coffeegrounds”) in stool.
• Lower GI bleeding: bleeding from the small intestine (jejunum or ileum), 
colon, or rectum (polyps, inflammatory bowel disease, diverticulosis, cancer, or 
hemorrhoids)
• Haematochezia (enterorrhagia): fresh blood in stool (ulcerative colitis, in 
the ileum and transverse colon or hemorrhoids)
• Haematochezia from an upper gastrointestinal source is an ominous 
sign, as it suggests a very significant bleed which is more likely to be 
life-threatening
• Rectorrhagia: rectal bleeding without defecation; expulsion of fresh bright 
red blood
• Occult bleeding is usually caused by slow, chronic blood loss that is not 
obvious and results in iron deficiency anemia as iron stores in the bone 
marrow are slowly depleted. 
• Acute, severe GI bleeding is life threatening depending on the volume and 
rate of blood loss, associated disease, age, and effectiveness of treatment.

Gastroesophageal Reflux Disease
Gastroesophageal Reflux Disease: the regurgitation of acid and pepsin from 
the stomach to the esophagus that causes esophagitis.
• Physiologic reflux: Gastroesophageal reflux that does not cause 
symptoms
• Nonerosive reflux disease: symptoms of reflux disease but no visible 
esophageal mucosal injury
Causes and risk factors: weaken low esophageal sphincter (LES) mediated by 
• Hiatal hernia
• Drugs or chemicals that relax the LES: anticholinergics, nitrates, calcium 
channel blockers, nicotine
• Increases abdominal pressure: vomiting, coughing, lifting, bending, or 
obesity or asthma or chronic cough
Later the inflammation can further damage vagus innervation that lead to delay 
gastric emptying worsening motility: lengthened period during which reflux is 
possible or increasing the acid content of chyme, e.g. gastric or duodenal 
ulcers, which can cause pyloric edema

Stress ulcer or Stress-Related Mucosal Disease
• An acute form of peptic ulcer
• Cause: the physiologic stress of severe illness; multisystem organ failure; 
or major trauma, including severe burns or head injury. Usually, multiple 
sites of ulceration are distributed within the stomach or duodenum.
• Stress ulcers may be classified as:
• Ischemic ulcers: within hours of an event(hemorrhage, multisystem 
trauma, severe burns, heart failure, or sepsis) that causes ischemia 
of the stomach and duodenal mucosa. Stress ulcers that develop as 
a result of burn injury are often called Curling ulcers.
• Cushing ulcers: named after Harvey Cushing, is a gastric ulcer 
associated with elevated intracranial pressure. Cushing ulcer results 
from decreased mucosal blood flow and hypersecretion of HCL 
caused by overstimulation of the vagal nuclei after severe head 
trauma or brain surgery.

Diverticular Disease of the Colon
Diverticula: herniation or saclike outpouchings of mucosa through the muscle 
layers of the colon wall. 
• anywhere in the gastrointestinal tract but the most common site is the left 
colon. They rarely occur in the small intestine
• The diverticula form at weak points in the colon wall, usually where arteries 
penetrate the tunica muscularis to nourish the mucosal layer.
• A common associated finding is thickening of the circular and longitudinal 
(teniae coli) muscles surrounding the diverticula. Hypertrophy and contraction 
of these muscles increase intraluminal pressure and degree of herniation.
Diverticulosis: asymptomatic diverticular disease. 
Diverticulitis represents inflammation. 
Causes: unknown but is associated with age greater than 60 years, combination
of decreased dietary fiber, increased intracolonic pressure, abnormal
neuromuscular function, and alterations in intestinal motility

Irritable Bowel Syndrome
• Abdominal pain and altered bowel habits (diarrhea-predominant,

• Common in women, with a higher prevalence in youth and middle age.

• Pathophysiology:
• Visceral hypersensitivity or hyperalgesia, particularly with

• Abnormal gastrointestinal permeability, motility, and secretion: The 
mechanisms may be related dysregulation of the brain-gut axis or 
to the role of serotonin
• Overgrowth of intestinal flora
• Food allergy or food intolerance
• Psychosocial factors

Ulcerative colitis (UC)
• UC is a chronic inflammatory disease that causes ulceration of the colonic 
mucosa and extends proximally from the rectum into the colon: 
continuous lesions, are limited to the mucosa, and are not transmural. The 
mucous layer is thinner than normal bud edema and thickening of the 
muscular mucosae may narrow the lumen of the involved colon. 
• In chronic disease, inflammatory polyps (pseudopolyps) develop in the 
colon from rapidly regenerating epithelium(also in CD). Mucosal 
destruction causes bloody diarrhea with passage of purulent mucus but 
severe hemorrhage is rare.
• T cells, plasma cells in the inflamed colon and colonic epithelial antibodies 
IgG in the circulation
• Its causes are unknown: bacterial, genetic, immunologic or dietary factors
