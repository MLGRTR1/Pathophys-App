Basal metabolic rate 
• refers to the amount of calories that your body needs to perform 
basic, life-sustaining functions. 
• It is the amount of energy expended at rest and neutral temperature. 
It is also necessary for the digestive system to be inactive, which 
requires at least a 12-hour fast. This number tells how much energy 
you need to sustain your vital organs like your brain, kidney, liver, 
heart, lungs, skin, muscles and your nervous system.
• It tells you how many calories your body would burn in a day if you 
do absolutely nothing and lie in bed.

=== PDF PAGE 16 ===
• The glycemic index is a value assigned to foods based on how quickly those foods 
cause increases in blood glucose levels. Foods low on the glycemic index (GI) scale 
tend to release glucose slowly and steadily. Foods with high glycemic index increase 
blood glucose rapidly. For instance, we would need to consume more beets than rice 
to cause the same spike in blood sugar levels.
• In particular, we measure the incremental area under the two-hour blood glucose 
response curve (AUC) following ingestion of a food with 50g of carbohydrate. The 
AUC of the test food is divided by the AUC of glucose (standard) and multiplied by 
100the.
• Foods with a high GI are not necessarily unhealthy, and not all foods with a low GI 
are healthy. E.g.  Fat and protein in a specific food can slow down carbohydrate 
absorption.

Nitrogen balance
• Nitrogen Balance = Nitrogen intake - Nitrogen loss (urinal urea and 
creatinine)
• Blood urea nitrogen (BUN) level and creatinine can be used in 
estimating nitrogen balance and can be used to study protein 
metabolism.
– Positive nitrogen balance: growth, hypothyroidism, tissue repair, and 
pregnancy.
– Negative nitrogen balance: burns, serious tissue injuries, 
fevers, hyperthyroidism, wasting diseases, during periods of 
fasting. A negative nitrogen balance can be used as part of a clinical 
evaluation of malnutrition.

• Creatinine is a breakdown product of creatine phosphate from muscle and

• Creatine in meat is converted to creatinine on cooking, which is 
absorbed, causing  increased serum creatinine.
• ↓BUN levels indicate malnutrition and ↑ BUN indicates increased protein 
released from muscle tissue breakdown (autolysis) that occurs during 
starvation to provide an energy source.
• The BUN and creatinine is important for assessment of kidney function: ↑blood

Refeeding syndrome
• Hypophosphatemia (a biochemical hallmark), abnormal sodium and fluid balance; 
changes in glucose, protein, and fat metabolism; thiamine deficiency; 
hypokalemia; and hypomagnesemia.
• Oral feeding resulted in fatal diarrhea, vomiting, heart failure and neurological 
complication (coma).
• Diarrhea may occur during the early stages of refeeding, due to atrophy of the intestinal mucosa and pancreatic 
impairment. The diarrhea generally resolves within the first few weeks of refeeding as the villous surface is 
reconstituted
• To prevent refeeding syndrome, initial fluid and electrolyte balance should be 
achieved prior to starting caloric intake. Once calories are started, the goal should 
be daily caloric requirements for age and weight divided into small frequent meals 
for the first one to two weeks.

Malassimilation syndrome
• Malassimilation is the decreased ability of the GI tract to incorporate 
nutrients into the body, either due to maldigestion or malabsorption.
• Primary: Ciliac disease, Tropical sprue, Whipple's disease, 
Lactase deficiency or lactose intolerance 
• Secondary: Pancreatic insufficiency and Bile salt insufficiency

Ciliac disease, Celiac sprue, nontropical sprue, 
endemic sprue, gluten enteropathy
• Cause: abnormal reaction to gluten (gliadins and glutenins in corns or

• Symptoms: 
• pale (anemia: iron, folic acid and vitamin B12 malabsorption )
• calcium and vitamin D malabsorption (and compensatory

• decreased vitamin K leading to petechia (hemostasis) 
• selenium malabsorption and copper and zinc deficiencies
• loose (large volume), abnormally bad smelling and greasy stool 
(steatorrhoea) leading to weight loss or failure to gain weight
• abdominal pain, cramping, bloating with abdominal distension (due to 
fermentative production of bowel gas)

adenocarcinoma and lymphoma

=== PDF PAGE 34 ===
Pancreatic insufficiency
• Causes of pancreatic insufficiency include chronic pancreatitis, 
pancreatic carcinoma, pancreatic resection and cystic fibrosis.
• Although pancreatic insufficiency causes poor digestion of all 
nutrients, fat maldigestion is the chief problem. A large amount of fat 
in the stool (steatorrhea) and weight loss are the most common 
signs.

=== PDF PAGE 35 ===
Bile salt insufficiency
• Causes of bile salt insufficiency include 
– advanced liver disease, which decreases production of bile salts 
– obstruction of the common bile duct, which decreases flow of 
bile into the duodenum; 
– intestinal stasis (lack of motility), which permits overgrowth of 
intestinal bacteria that deconjugate bile salts; 
– diseases of the ileum, which prevent the reabsorption and 
recycling of bile salts (enterohepatic circulation)
• Sign: poor intestinal absorption of fat (steatorrhea) and fat-soluble 
vitamins (A, D, E, and K).

Other courses of malassimilation: Crohn disease
• CD affects any part of the gastrointestinal tract from the mouth to the anus. 
The distal small intestine (malabsorption) and proximal large intestine are 
most commonly affected (patchy distribution in the colon or ileum).
• The typical chronic lesion is a skip granuloma having cobblestone 
projections of inflamed tissue surrounded by areas of ulceration. It starts in 
the intestinal submucosa and spreads across the intestinal wall to involve 
the mucosa and serosa in areas overlying lymphoid tissue. Complications 
include obstruction, fistulae, abscess formation, and chronic blood loss.
• Th1 immune response and IgG associated with severity
• Its causes are unknown: aggressive response to normal flora bacteria in 
genetically predisposed individuals (e.g. TLR)

Other courses of malassimilation: Ulcerative colitis 
(UC)
• UC is a chronic inflammatory disease that causes ulceration of the 
colonic mucosa and extends proximally from the rectum into the 
colon: continuous lesions, are limited to the mucosa, and are not 
transmural. The mucous layer is thinner than normal bud edema and 
thickening of the muscular mucosae may narrow the lumen of the 
involved colon. 
• In chronic disease, inflammatory polyps (pseudopolyps) develop in 
the colon from rapidly regenerating epithelium(also in CD). Mucosal 
destruction causes bloody diarrhea with passage of purulent mucus 
but severe hemorrhage is rare.
• T cells, plasma cells in the inflamed colon and colonic epithelial 
antibodies IgG in the circulation
• Its causes are unknown: bacterial, genetic, immunologic or dietary
factors
